## The five concepts

### 1 · People Used to Sleep Twice a Night. Then We Forgot. (angle 1)
- **Thumbnail:** a night-blue bar across the frame reading SLEEP · ??? · SLEEP, the gap glowing amber, over a candle-lit paper
  bedroom. Text: THE HOUR WE LOST.
- **Premise:** Diaries, court records and medical books from pre-industrial Europe describe a "first sleep" and a "second sleep"
  with an hour or more awake in between, and what people did in that hour runs from prayer to sex to burglary, so the film is one
  night that keeps handing over stranger answers. The loop that carries it is the viewer's own 3 a.m. wake-up: we promise early to
  say whether it is a relic or a problem, and pay it late with the checks.
- **How it's told:** one night, hour by hour, in the second person.
- **Hook (first 15 s):** a 16th-century doctor's advice on the best time to conceive, "after the first sleep", read from the page.
- **Facts**

  | Claim | Source | Status |
  |---|---|---|
  | "First sleep" and "second sleep" appear in 500+ references across ancient, medieval and early-modern documents | A. R. Ekirch, *At Day's Close* (2005); his 2016 letter in *Sleep* (PMC4763365) | verified (letter) |
  | With 14 hours of darkness a night, lab subjects' sleep split into two bouts with 1–3 hours awake between | Wehr 1992, *J. Sleep Res.* | verified via secondary; sample size TO CHECK |
  | Three foraging societies near the equator showed no segmented sleep | Yetish et al. 2015, *Current Biology* | verified via Ekirch's letter |
  | In a Hadza camp everyone was asleep at once for only 18 minutes in 20 days; a median of 8 people were awake at night | Samson et al. 2017, *Proc. R. Soc. B* 284:20170967 | verified (abstract) |
  | What people did between sleeps; the doctor's advice; court depositions | Ekirch 2001 (*AHR*) and 2005 | from memory, TO CHECK |
  | Night-time wakefulness and worse judgment ("mind after midnight") | Tubbs et al. 2022 | from memory, TO CHECK |
- **Risks:** a health topic, so no medical advice and a "see a professional" line. The title says "people", not "humans", because
  the hunter-gatherer data cuts against "natural". An old idea used "3 AM" for a different topic (replaying embarrassing moments);
  this one does not use a bedside clock.

### 2 · Can I Give You a False Memory in 60 Seconds? (angle 1)
- **Thumbnail:** a torn paper word list (BED · REST · TIRED · DREAM …) with one empty line circled in red marker. Text: IT WAS
  NEVER HERE.
- **Premise:** The viewer is the experiment: a word list in the first seconds, a test two minutes later, and for many viewers a
  confident memory of a word that was never shown, so they stay to find out how it was done to them. Then the stakes climb, from
  a party trick to students whose memory of the Challenger explosion had changed two and a half years later, to what that does to
  eyewitness testimony.
- **How it's told:** the viewer is tested live, then shown the mechanism.
- **Hook (first 15 s):** "I'm going to read you fifteen words. Don't write them down."
- **Facts**

  | Claim | Source | Status |
  |---|---|---|
  | Lists of related words make people recall a related word that was never presented | Deese 1959; Roediger & McDermott 1995, *JEP: LMC* | known; recall rates (about 40–55%) TO CHECK |
  | Memories of hearing about Challenger had deteriorated significantly 2.5 years later | Neisser & Harsch 1992, in *Affect and Accuracy in Recall* | verified broadly; the sample (106, 44 retested), scores, confidence and the "that's my handwriting" quote TO CHECK |
  | Whole false childhood events can be planted in a minority of people | Loftus & Pickrell 1995; Wade et al. 2002; Scoboria et al. 2017 | from memory, TO CHECK |
  | Mistaken eyewitnesses and wrongful convictions | Innocence Project figures | TO CHECK, refresh before air |
- **Risks:** the effect does not work on everyone, which is why the title is a question. Tragedies are handled plainly and
  briefly. NASA material is public domain.

### 3 · The Man Who Named Stockholm Syndrome Never Spoke to the Hostage (angle 2)
- **Thumbnail:** a bank-vault door, a telephone receiver hanging by its cord, a DIAGNOSIS stamp. Text: HE NEVER ASKED HER.
- **Premise:** It opens inside a Stockholm bank vault in August 1973, with a hostage on the phone to Sweden's prime minister
  saying she is more afraid of the police than of the robbers, and it runs as a six-day siege before it turns on the famous
  label. The pay-off is the paper trail: the psychiatrist advising the police coined the term without, by her account and later
  reporting, speaking to her; it has never been an official diagnosis; and an FBI report found signs of it in about 8% of hostage
  cases.
- **How it's told:** the siege in order, then the diagnosis put on trial.
- **Hook (first 15 s):** the phone call.
- **Facts**

  | Claim | Source | Status |
  |---|---|---|
  | 23–28 Aug 1973, Kreditbanken on Norrmalmstorg; four hostages; ended when police used gas | History.com; Wikipedia | verified; TO CHECK against Swedish sources |
  | Kristin Enmark phoned PM Olof Palme and said she feared the police, not the robbers | Sveriges Radio; History.com; ABC News 2023 | verified; wording differs by translation, so quote from the recording, TO CHECK |
  | Nils Bejerot coined "Norrmalmstorgssyndromet" and never spoke to Enmark | ABC News (Aus.) 2023; other reporting | reported; second independent source TO CHECK |
  | Not an official psychiatric diagnosis | DSM-5-TR / ICD-11 | TO CHECK wording |
  | About 8% of hostage victims showed signs (5% excluding those only negative toward police) | 1999 FBI report, via secondary sources; FBI Law Enforcement Bulletin, July 2007 | secondary only; original TO CHECK |
  | A review found no validated diagnostic criteria | Namnyak et al. 2008, *Acta Psychiatr. Scand.* | from memory, TO CHECK |
  | Enmark disputes the diagnosis; an older Swedish Radio piece quotes her as partly accepting it | her book; Sveriges Radio | both sides to be shown |
- **Risks:** real people, some living. Credited photos only, framed neutrally; every allegation attributed; the police's and
  Bejerot's own account shown. The satire is aimed at the label and at official language, never at a named person's character.

### 4 · The Shock Experiment Proves the Opposite of What You Were Taught (angle 2)
- **Thumbnail:** the shock machine's row of switches ending at 450 VOLTS, and a typed script line, "You have no other choice",
  struck through in red. Text: 0 OBEYED.
- **Premise:** Everyone knows the lesson, that ordinary people will electrocute a stranger if a man in a lab coat tells them to,
  so the hook is one sentence from the experimenter's script: the only line that was a real order, which we promise to come back
  to. At the midpoint it pays: in a 2009 replication nobody who was given that order went on, and across Milgram's own versions
  most people disobeyed, which leaves a better and darker question: if not orders, what did keep them going?
- **How it's told:** the four scripted lines as a ladder; the fourth is the twist.
- **Hook (first 15 s):** the fourth line on a typed page, covered, with the promise to uncover it.
- **Facts**

  | Claim | Source | Status |
  |---|---|---|
  | The experimenter had four scripted prods; only the fourth is plainly an order | Milgram 1963/1974; commentary | verified |
  | 2009 replication: 64% continued after prod 1, 46% after prod 2, 10% after prod 3, none after prod 4 | Burger, Girgensohn & Neubaum 2011, via FORRT and BPS Research Digest | verified via reviews; original TO CHECK |
  | The caveat: prod 4 always followed three refusals, and few people reached it | same commentary | must be said on screen |
  | A restaging found near-universal refusal after prod 4 | Haslam, Reicher & Millard 2015, *PLoS ONE* | verified (abstract) |
  | Across Milgram's variations obedience ran from 0% to 92.5% and averaged 43% | Haslam, Loughnan & Perry 2014, via secondary | TO CHECK original |
  | In an unpublished test, 56% defied at some point, and those who believed the learner was hurt defied more | Perry, Brannigan, Wanner & Stam 2019, *Soc. Psych. Quarterly* | verified (abstract, press) |
- **Risks:** the Yale film and photos are copyrighted, so the room is a drawn reconstruction and the documents are quoted text.

### 5 · Why It's So Hard to Cancel Anything (angle 3)
- **Thumbnail:** a giant green SUBSCRIBE button beside a tiny grey "cancel" link under a magnifying glass. Text: NOT AN ACCIDENT.
- **Premise:** It starts with the viewer's own bank statement ("you are paying for something right now that you forgot") and
  follows one cancellation, screen by screen, naming the lever each screen pulls. The receipts keep it moving: 7,752 gym members
  who paid over $17 a visit when $10 was on offer and took 2.3 months to cancel after their last workout, and a cancellation
  process that, the US regulator said, a company's own staff nicknamed "Iliad", after the epic about a ten-year war, before a
  $2.5 billion settlement in 2025.
- **How it's told:** one cancellation taken apart screen by screen.
- **Hook (first 15 s):** the viewer's bank statement, one line circled.
- **Facts**

  | Claim | Source | Status |
  |---|---|---|
  | 7,752 members, three US clubs; monthly members paying over $70 paid more than $17 per expected visit against a $10 pass; 2.31 months from last visit to cancelling; about $600 forgone | DellaVigna & Malmendier 2006, *AER* 96(3) | verified (paper) |
  | The FTC said Amazon's Prime cancellation process was internally called "Iliad" | FTC complaint, June 2023; NBC, PBS, Fortune | verified (press); complaint TO CHECK |
  | Settlement of 25 Sept 2025: $2.5 billion ($1 billion penalty, $1.5 billion refunds) | NBC, PBS, Fortune | verified; Amazon's statement and whether it admitted wrongdoing TO CHECK |
  | "Four pages, six clicks, fifteen options" to cancel | FTC complaint | from memory, TO CHECK |
  | The FTC's click-to-cancel rule was struck down on 8 July 2025; the FTC restarted the rulemaking in March 2026 | Jones Day, Gibson Dunn notes | verified; refresh before air |
  | India's 2023 dark-pattern guidelines list the "subscription trap" | CCPA guidelines | from memory, TO CHECK |
- **Risks:** a named company, so the allegations are attributed and its reply is shown. Advice is general consumer information.
