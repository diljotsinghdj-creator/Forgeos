PHONE SHOOT LIST - SHORTS 13 TO 24
Faceless shots you can film at home on a phone. Each one replaces the picture(s) under the spoken line named
(quoted exactly). Film 9:16 (phone upright), steady (prop the phone on books or a mug), daylight from a window,
no faces, no readable personal details (blur or turn over real cards, letters and screens). Hold each shot 3 s
unless noted; film 2-3 takes. Lines that carry a full-frame graphic (Chart, Steps, Screen, Statement, Document...)
are left out on purpose - the graphic covers the picture there.

== 13 Why You Cannot Stop Scrolling
1. "You checked the weather. Twenty minutes ago."  Close, top-down: your hand holding your phone on a weather
   page (screen dimmed / out of focus), then tilt up to a wall or oven clock. 3 s.
2. "Now picture a book. Chapters end. Pages run out."  Top-down: a hand closing a paperback on a table. 3 s.
3. "Those are stopping cues, moments to decide whether to continue."  Close: a spoon scraping the bottom of an
   empty cereal bowl. 3 s. (Pair with shot 4 for the Split.)
4. "Those are stopping cues, moments to decide whether to continue."  Close: TV going to black / a laptop lid
   closing in a dim room. 3 s.
5. "There isn't one."  Wide, low angle: a dark living room, one lamp on, phone glowing face up on the sofa. 3 s.
6. "Set a timer before you open the app. Your own last page."  Close: hand twisting a kitchen timer, then
   placing the phone face down beside it. 3-4 s.
7. "Then check the weather. That's what you came for."  Close: rain on a window, or a mug of tea next to a
   folded umbrella. 3 s.

== 14 The Man Who Sold the Eiffel Tower  (Archive line - film plain, the old-film look is added; no phones or
   modern objects in frame)
1. "He sold the Eiffel Tower he never owned."  Top-down: a sheet of old-looking paper (tea-stained) slid across
   a wooden table, text out of focus. 3 s.
2. "Con man Victor Lustig posed as a fake government official."  Close: a hand pressing a wax seal or a rubber
   stamp onto paper. 3 s.
3. "He even hinted at a bribe. That made him seem like a real official."  Close, side-on: a gloved hand sliding
   a thick envelope across a table. 3 s.
4. "One dealer, André Poisson, paid up. Lustig fled with the cash."  Top-down: hands counting a stack of old
   banknotes or play money. 3 s.
5. "So why did the police never hear about it?"  Close: a blank form and a pen lying untouched on a desk. 3 s.
6. "The tower never moved. The money never came back."  Top-down: an empty leather wallet dropped open on a
   wooden table. 3 s. (Pair with any tower photo for the Split.)

== 15 Why Notifications Work
1. "One buzz. Twenty minutes gone. Who decided that?"  Close: a phone face up on a dark table lighting up (set
   an alarm), screen out of focus. 3 s.
2. "Nobody even touched the phone. So what happens when you do?"  Top-down: phone face down beside a crossword,
   the pen put down. 3 s.
3. "You tap for one thing. The app shows you ten more."  Close: a cup of tea going cold on a side table, steam
   fading. 3 s.
4. "Is it the alerts, or is it you? One test tells you."  Close: phone face up with the lock screen full of
   blurred alerts. 3 s. (First half of the Split.)
5. "Then count how many silenced apps you actually missed."  Top-down: a paper calendar, a pencil crossing off
   seven days, then tally marks on a notepad. 4 s.
6. "One buzz shouldn't cost twenty minutes. Now it doesn't have to."  Wide: a sunny desk with an open book and
   a coffee, phone face down at the edge. 3 s.

== 16 The Small Yes That Leads to a Big Yes  (lines 1-5 are Archive lines: keep modern objects out of frame)
1. "A huge, ugly lawn sign? Most said no."  Low angle: a big cardboard sign propped crookedly in a garden or
   against a wall. 3 s.
2. "Then one tiny favour, weeks earlier, changed the answer."  Close: a pencil crossing weeks off a wall
   calendar. 3 s.
3. "A small sign in the window, about safe driving."  Close, from outside or inside: a small card taped to a
   window corner, writing out of focus. 3 s.
4. "Why? Saying yes once changes how you see yourself."  Close: a hand dropping a tin into a donation bag or box. 3 s.
5. "Sales funnels often start the same way, with a free quiz or tiny trial."  Top-down: a free sample sachet
   on a magazine page. 3 s. (Second half of the Split.)
6. "So next time a tiny yes arrives, ask what the big one will be."  Close, from behind: a hand pressing a
   doorbell. 3 s.

== 17 The Stranger Who Texted the Wrong Number
1. "A wrong-number text. Weeks later, savings gone."  Close: an empty piggy bank or jar tipped on its side on
   a kitchen table. 3 s.
2. "The stranger apologises, keeps chatting, and becomes a friend for weeks."  Top-down: a desk calendar with
   weeks crossed off, a phone face down beside it. 3 s.
3. "So why can't you withdraw a penny?"  Close: a locked cash box or tin, a hand trying the lid. 3 s.
4. "Every withdrawal needs another fee, until you stop paying."  Top-down: a pile of printed sheets, each
   circled in red pen (text out of focus), then an empty wallet. 4 s. (Split pair.)
5. "Never invest on advice from someone you've only met online."  Close: two hands placing a phone face down
   on a table. 3 s.
6. "Especially someone who found you by accident."  Top-down: a letter lying on a doormat, address hidden. 3 s.

== 18 Why Bad News Grabs Attention
1. "One scary headline beats a hundred calm days."  Top-down: a newspaper with a dark front page, headlines
   out of focus, then a calm sunny window. 3 s each. (Split pair.)
2. "Their bodies told the real story. Bad news hit harder than good."  Close: a fingertip with a clothes-peg or
   pulse clip on it resting on a table. 3 s.
3. "So why does bad news still feel everywhere?"  Close: a phone face up with a stack of blurred alerts. 3 s.
4. "Vivid stories come to mind easily, so they only feel common."  Close, slow pan: newspaper clippings pinned
   to a corkboard or fridge, text unreadable. 3 s.
5. "But here's the catch."  Close: a hand holding one newspaper clipping up to window light. 3 s.
6. "A true story can still say nothing about odds."  Top-down: a single die rolled onto a wooden table under a
   lamp. 3 s.

== 19 The Cost of Panic Buying
1. "Four minutes to buy it. Zero pages read."  Top-down: a thick unopened booklet or folder on a kitchen table,
   a phone beside it. 3 s.
2. "So you picture a freezing house, and you tap buy."  Close: a thumb tapping a phone screen (screen out of
   focus), or frost on a window. 3 s.
3. "Reading the exclusions, checking the excess, comparing the price."  Close: a magnifying glass moving over
   fine print, then two printed sheets side by side. 4 s. (Split pair.)
4. "So can you undo a four-minute decision?"  Close: an egg timer tipped on its side on a table. 3 s.
5. "There is a catch. Exceptions apply, so read your terms next morning."  Top-down: a printed page with one
   paragraph circled in red pen, a coffee in morning light. 3 s.
6. "Four minutes to buy. Often fourteen days to rethink. Use them."  Wide: phone face down on a kitchen table,
   then a bed with the duvet turned back under a lamp. 4 s.

== 20 The Call That Sounded Like Your Child
1. "Your daughter's voice, crying. But it isn't her."  Close: an old phone handset off the hook, or a phone
   face down buzzing on a dark table. 3 s.
2. "Scammers can clone a voice from only a short online clip."  Close: a phone on a mini tripod filming an
   empty sofa, screen out of focus. 3 s.
3. "The panic is real. So what can you trust?"  Close: a hand gripping a phone tightly, screen dark. 3 s.
4. "Instead, hang up and call her on the number you already know."  Close: thumb on the red end-call button
   (screen blurred), then a hand flipping a paper address book. 4 s. (Split pair.)
5. "Agree a family code word today. A clone can't know it."  Top-down: a hand folding a small paper note into
   an envelope on a kitchen table. 3 s.
6. "Then any crying voice must pass one question first."  Close: a handset placed back on its cradle, or a phone
   set face down. 3 s.

== 21 The Authority Trap
1. "Same man, new clothes. Why do strangers obey?"  Close: a hand hanging a peaked cap or hi-vis vest on a coat
   hook. 3 s.
2. "As a guard, he was obeyed more than in plain clothes."  From behind, chest down: the same person in a
   dark uniform-style jacket, then in a hoodie, same spot. 3 s each. (Split pair; Archive line - plain wall,
   no modern cars or phones in frame.)
3. "A guard had no power here. So why?"  Close: two empty hands opened palms up against a wall. 3 s.
4. "So scammers borrow them, like the tax office's crest."  Top-down: a pile of official-looking envelopes on a
   doormat, crests and names hidden. 3 s.
5. "The real office never texts you about a refund."  Close: a phone lying dark on a kitchen table beside a mug. 3 s.
6. "Same request, new clothes. Judge the request, not the uniform."  Close: a jacket hanging by the door, then a
   hand holding a printed sheet up to a lamp, text unreadable. 4 s.

== 22 How Scammers Create Urgency
1. "Ten minutes, or your savings are gone."  Close: a phone ringing face up on a dark desk (screen blurred),
   an empty jar beside it. 3 s.
2. "Why the clock? A deadline leaves no time to check."  Close: an old alarm clock ticking; then keys grabbed
   from a hallway hook. 4 s.
3. "Real banks never ask that. The account is the scammer's."  Top-down: a bank leaflet on a counter (text
   unreadable), then someone walking away down a hallway, legs only. 4 s. (Split pair.)
4. "And the cost?"  Top-down: an open, empty purse on a kitchen table under a lamp. 2 s.
5. "Hang up. Call a number you look up yourself."  Close: a hand setting a phone face down; a finger on the back
   of a bank card with digits covered. 4 s.
6. "Ten minutes? The real bank will still be there tomorrow."  Close: an alarm clock on a bedside table in
   morning light. 3 s.

== 23 The Video Call Where Everyone Was Fake
1. "Every face was fake. The money was real."  Top-down: a row of ID-style badges or cards turned face down,
   then a stack of play money. 3 s each. (Split pair.)
2. "It claims to be the finance chief, wanting a secret transaction."  Close: a sealed envelope with a red sticker
   on a desk. 3 s.
3. "So who would doubt faces they recognise?"  Over-the-shoulder, no faces: a laptop showing a video call
   grid, the screen deliberately out of focus. 3 s.
4. "Every other person on that call was a deepfake."  Wide: empty chairs around a dining table in a dark room. 3 s.
5. "The fix? Check any money request on a separate channel."  Close: a hand picking up a landline or a second
   phone; or a knuckle knocking on a door. 3 s.

== 24 The Question That Breaks the Trap
1. "Three traps. One question stops them all."  Top-down: three phones (or phone-sized cards) side by side on a
   dark desk, then a flat hand raised in a stop gesture. 3 s.
2. "Who benefits if I act right now?"  Top-down: a pen writing one line on a notepad, words out of focus. 3 s.
3. "Watch it work on all three."  Close: a finger tapping the first of three phones lined up on a desk. 3 s.
4. "The midnight buzz? The app. Your attention is what it sells."  Close: a phone glowing on a pillow in a dark
   bedroom, screen blurred. 3 s. (First half of the Split.)
5. "Only a scammer needs you to skip checking."  Wide: a dark doorway, a coat on a hook, nobody in frame. 3 s.
6. "Who benefits if I act right now? Ask it every time."  Close: a hand pausing on a front door handle, then
   three phones face down in morning light. 4 s.
