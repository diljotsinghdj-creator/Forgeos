A vivid threat can crowd out the question of how likely it is.
Visual: A blurred television glowing in a dark living room, seen from behind a sofa

And that blind spot turns out to be very old, and very consistent.

In nineteen seventy-eight, researchers asked people to estimate how often different things kill.
Visual: Hands holding a pencil over a paper questionnaire, rows of boxes, text unreadable
Text: Lichtenstein et al., 1978

Judgements leaned towards dramatic, memorable causes and away from quiet, common ones.
Visual: Two stacks of index cards, one tall and one short, on a dark table

Warned about the bias and asked to correct for it, most still could not.
Visual: An eraser rubbing at a pencil mark that will not fully disappear, macro
Text: WARNED, STILL BIASED

A follow-up compared those distorted estimates with newspaper coverage of each cause.
Visual: A pile of folded newspapers on a dark table, headlines blurred
Stock: stack of newspapers

The estimates tracked how often each cause made the papers, not how often it killed.
Visual: Scissors resting on a newspaper clipping, macro, print unreadable
Text: Combs & Slovic, 1979

A later paper by Cass Sunstein and Richard Zeckhauser tested the same blind spot with money.
Visual: Hands counting a few banknotes on a kitchen table, faces out of frame
Text: Sunstein & Zeckhauser

When a cancer risk was described vividly, what people would pay to remove it barely moved.
Visual: A glass of tap water on a dark windowsill, light catching the surface

Even when the risk itself was ten times larger.
Visual: Ten identical glasses of water in a row, one lit, the rest in shadow
Text: 10× THE RISK, SAME PRICE

The same authors describe what fear does next. They call it action bias.
Visual: A hand gripping a door handle in a dim hallway, close-up

When an outcome frightens us, doing something feels better than doing nothing.
Visual: Fingers drumming restlessly on a dark wooden table
Text: ACTION BIAS

And buying something is the easiest something there is.
Visual: A thumb hovering over a glowing phone screen, button area unreadable

Now think about what that means for anyone with something to sell.
Visual: A darkened shop window at night, empty display stand lit by a single bulb

If fear makes probability fade, fear becomes a way to set the price.
Visual: A price tag hanging from a string, swinging slowly in lamp light, text unreadable

In a calm market, sellers compete on what the cover is actually worth.
Visual: Three blank price cards in a row on a shop counter, even lighting

A frightened buyer compares less and asks fewer questions about price.
Visual: Two of the price cards slipping into shadow, only one still lit

So the contest shifts away from value, towards whoever frightens best.
Visual: A single spotlight on one product box in an otherwise dark room

Why is that order so effective? A huge body of research has measured it.

Watch for the shift from could happen to will happen to you.
