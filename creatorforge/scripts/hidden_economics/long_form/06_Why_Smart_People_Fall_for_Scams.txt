Knowing a lot about money can make some scams work better on you.
Visual: A hand resting on a stack of finance books beside a glowing phone, cold blue night light
Text: KNOWLEDGE CAN BACKFIRE

That is not a hunch. It came from research for a UK regulator.
Visual: A thick bound report lying on a dark desk, cover blank, lit by a phone screen
Text: OFT / University of Exeter, 2009

Psychologists studied scam victims for the Office of Fair Trading.
Visual: Overhead shot of research papers spread across a table, text unreadable

They found that background knowledge of a scam's subject could raise the risk.
Visual: A pile of investment brochures on a coffee table, logos absent

People who knew lotteries or investments were sometimes easier to catch, not harder.
Visual: A lottery-style ticket and a share certificate side by side, printing blurred
Text: MORE KNOWLEDGE, MORE RISK?

And many victims were not poor decision-makers. Some had successful careers.
Visual: A smart jacket hanging on the back of an office chair in a dark room

So if intelligence does not protect you, what is actually going wrong?
Visual: A silhouetted figure standing at a window at night, phone in hand
Text: WHAT GOES WRONG?

Start with who gets caught, because the stereotype is upside down.
Visual: An empty park bench under a streetlight at night, wide shot

In America, the Federal Trade Commission logs millions of fraud reports.
Visual: Rows of filing cabinets in a dim archive, one drawer open
Text: FTC Consumer Sentinel

In twenty twenty-four, people in their twenties reported a loss in forty-four percent of reports.

Text: AGE 20–29: 44% lost money

People in their seventies reported a loss in twenty-four percent.
Visual: Older hands resting on a closed laptop at a kitchen table
Text: AGE 70–79: 24% lost money

Older victims tend to lose more per case. Younger ones lose money more often.
Visual: One tall stack of coins and many small stacks, side by side on a dark table
Text: FTC data, 2024

In fact, about three quarters of older adults' reports showed no money lost at all.
Visual: Older hands placing a phone face down on a tablecloth, calm daylight
Text: ~74% of older adults' reports: no loss

And the overall bill keeps climbing.

Text: $15.9bn reported lost, 2025

The biggest single slice went to investment scams.
Visual: A generic trading chart glowing on a laptop in a dark room, figures blurred
Text: Investment scams: $7.9bn

Investment is exactly the subject where people feel most knowledgeable.

The same pattern shows up in Britain, as the UK figures will show.
Visual: A rain-streaked window with a blurred British street beyond, night

So scams are not really a problem of age, or of intelligence.

They are a problem of situation.

What does that situation look like? Picture a fictional phone call.

Text: THE NUMBER MATCHES

Text: PROOF?

It does not. Ofcom warns that criminals can spoof the number you see.
Visual: Two identical phones side by side, both glowing, one in shadow
Text: NUMBERS CAN BE SPOOFED

A call can seem to come from your bank, the tax office or a courier.

Visual: Hands holding a phone with a generic transfer screen, text unreadable
Text: A SAFE ACCOUNT

Notice what has just happened in under a minute.

Authority, fear and a deadline have arrived together.
Visual: Three dominoes on a dark table toppling in sequence, macro
Text: AUTHORITY + FEAR + DEADLINE

Each one makes checking feel slower, riskier, even disloyal.

So why would knowledge make this worse rather than better?
Visual: A bookshelf of finance titles in shadow, spines unreadable
Text: WHY KNOWLEDGE BACKFIRES

The Exeter researchers pointed to over-confidence.
Visual: A chess piece standing alone on a board under a single lamp

If you know the subject, a scam can feel like a conversation you understand.
Visual: Two coffee cups on a table, one person's hands visible, faceless

Commentary on the report suggested knowledgeable people engaged with approaches others ignored.
Visual: A hand opening an envelope at a kitchen table while others lie unopened

They read further, weighed it up, and stayed on the line.
Visual: A phone held to an ear in silhouette against a blue window

Fluency feels like safety. But the scam does not need you to be foolish.
Visual: A smooth stone skimming across dark water, slow motion
Stock: stone skipping water

It only needs you to keep talking instead of checking.
Visual: A phone screen showing a long call timer, digits blurred

Victims also tended to find it harder to control their emotions under pressure.
Visual: A hand gripping the edge of a kitchen counter, knuckles tense

So the real attack is not on what you know. It is on your pulse.
Visual: Macro of a wristwatch second hand moving, cold light
Text: THE TARGET IS YOUR PULSE

In other words, a few simple rules are working. Here they are.
Visual: A notepad and pen on a desk, a hand about to write

First, banks and police never ask you to move money to a safe account.
Visual: Phone screen showing a generic transfer form being closed, text unreadable
Text: NEVER A SAFE ACCOUNT

That single sentence is the backbone of the Take Five campaign.
Visual: A hand placing a single card face down on a table
Text: Take Five to Stop Fraud

Second, never share a one-time passcode, or approve a payment you did not start.
Visual: Phone showing a generic text message with a six-digit code, digits blurred
Text: NEVER SHARE THE CODE

Third, any request to keep a call secret from your bank is a warning sign.
Visual: A finger pressed to closed lips, silhouette against a blue window
Text: SECRECY = RED FLAG

And fourth, the hardest one. Only a criminal needs you to hurry.
Visual: A runner's feet frozen mid-stride on a wet pavement at night
Text: ONLY CRIMINALS RUSH YOU

So what do you actually do when the call comes?
Visual: The phone ringing again on the dark table, wide shot
Text: WHAT DO YOU DO?

Hang up. You are not being rude. You are breaking the script.
Visual: A thumb pressing the end-call area on a glowing phone, macro
Text: HANG UP

Then call your bank on a number you found yourself.
Visual: Hands turning a bank card over to its back, details blurred

The back of your card, your statement or the official app.
Visual: A card, a paper statement and a phone laid out on a table, text unreadable

In the UK there is also a three-digit shortcut. One five nine.
Visual: A finger dialling three digits on a phone keypad, numbers out of focus

Run by Stop Scams UK, it connects you to many major banks.
Visual: A landline phone on a hallway table, soft lamp light
