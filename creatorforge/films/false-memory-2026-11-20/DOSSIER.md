# Dossier: Can I Give You a False Memory in 60 Seconds?

Every fact the script may use, with its source and status. **Verified** means confirmed on 2026-11-20 by web search against the
paper's abstract, the publisher, or at least one reliable summary. The session could not open the papers themselves (web pages
are blocked here; only search works), so anything marked ⚠ is checked against the original before the voice is recorded.

**Rule for the script:** use only the numbers in the "Claim" column, worded no more strongly than they are here.

## Act 0–1 · The word list (the trap) and how it works
| ID | Claim | Source | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| D1 | Read people a list of related words (bed, rest, awake…) and many "remember" a related word that was never on it (sleep). In the 1995 study the missing word was recalled 40% of the time in experiment 1 and 55% in experiment 2, and on a later recognition test people picked it at a rate comparable to words they had really heard. | Roediger & McDermott 1995, "Creating false memories: Remembering words not presented in lists", *J. Exp. Psychol.: Learning, Memory, and Cognition* 21(4), 803–814 (abstract) | verified |
| D2 | The list for SLEEP, 15 words in this order: bed, rest, awake, tired, dream, wake, snooze, blanket, doze, slumber, snore, nap, peace, yawn, drowsy. | Roediger & McDermott 1995, appendix; Stadler, Roediger & McDermott 1999, *Memory & Cognition* | ⚠ from memory; confirm the words and order against the appendix |
| D2b | In later norms, 60% or more of people falsely recalled "sleep" after its list, and more than 80% falsely recognised it. Lists differ a lot: the weakest ("king") gave 10% and 27%. | Stadler, Roediger & McDermott 1999 | verified |
| D3 | James Deese found the effect in 1959 with 36 lists of 12 words. It was largely overlooked for 36 years until Roediger and McDermott revived it; hence "DRM". | Deese 1959, *J. Exp. Psychol.*; Roediger & McDermott 1995 | verified |
| D4 | Warning people in advance reduces the illusion but does not remove it. | Gallo, Roberts & Seamon 1997, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*; McDermott & Roediger 1998, *J. Memory and Language* | verified (abstracts) |
| D5 | Quote: "Memory works a little bit more like a Wikipedia page: you can go in there and change it, but so can other people." | Elizabeth Loftus, TEDGlobal 2013, "How reliable is your memory?" (transcript) | verified |
| D6 | People with highly superior autobiographical memory (20 of them, against 38 matched controls) were as susceptible to false memories as everyone else, including on word lists. The authors' conclusion, paraphrased (not a quotation): memories like theirs are rebuilt, as everyone's are. | Patihis et al. 2013, *PNAS* | verified |
| D19 | Why it happens, in plain terms: memory keeps the gist more than the exact words, and it is poor at telling "I heard it" from "I thought it". | Fuzzy-trace theory (Brainerd & Reyna); source monitoring (Johnson, Hashtroudi & Lindsay 1993) | textbook; keep it general, name no numbers |

## Act 2 · Challenger
| ID | Claim | Source | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| D7 | 28 January 1986: the space shuttle Challenger broke apart 73 seconds after launch, with seven crew aboard. | NASA | known; ⚠ confirm wording at NASA |
| D8b | Vivid memories of how you heard shocking news are known as "flashbulb memories". | Brown & Kulik 1977 (the term); the title of Neisser & Harsch's chapter | textbook term; from memory |
| D8 | The morning after, 106 Emory University psychology students wrote down how they had heard the news. About two and a half years later, 44 of them answered the same questions. Scored against their own first answers on a 7-point scale, the average was 2.95. Eleven scored zero and 22 scored 2 or less. Average confidence was 4.17 out of 5, and confidence was unrelated to accuracy. Shown their first answers in their own handwriting, most were surprised and most kept the new version. | Neisser & Harsch 1992, "Phantom flashbulbs", in Winograd & Neisser (eds.), *Affect and Accuracy in Recall* | verified through several summaries; ⚠ the chapter itself not seen |

**Not used (could not be confirmed):** the quote "That's my handwriting, but that's not what happened"; "three perfect scores"; the
shift toward "I saw it on TV".

## Act 3 · Whole events
| ID | Claim | Source | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| D9 | 24 people were given a booklet of childhood stories supplied by a relative: three true, one invented (lost in a shopping mall at about five). Six of them (25%) came to remember the invented one. | Loftus & Pickrell 1995, *Psychiatric Annals* 25(12), 720–725 | verified (via the 2023 replication) |
| D10 | A preregistered replication with 123 people coded 35% as reporting a false memory of being lost in a mall (8% full, 27% partial). | Murphy et al. 2023, *Memory* 31(6), 818–830 (University College Cork and University College Dublin) | verified |
| D11 | The other side. Critics who reanalysed that data argue about half of those reports may be real memories of really being lost, and count only 4% as full false memories; that reading is itself disputed. One review puts full false memories at about 15% of participants, with some recollection in 47%. A pooled analysis of 8 studies (423 people) classed 30.4% as false memories. | Andrews & Brewin 2024; Brewin & Andrews 2017, *Applied Cognitive Psychology*; Scoboria et al. 2017, *Memory* 25(2) | verified |
| D12 | Shown a doctored photo of themselves on a hot-air-balloon ride that never happened, 10 of 20 people came to remember at least part of it after three interviews. | Wade, Garry, Read & Lindsay 2002, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* | verified |

## Act 4 · The courtroom
| ID | Claim | Source | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| D15 | Of the 375 people cleared by DNA evidence in the US from 1989 to 2020, 69% had been convicted with the help of a mistaken eyewitness. They served 14 years on average. | Innocence Project, "DNA Exonerations in the United States (1989–2020)" | verified; refresh before air |
| D16 | July 1984, Burlington, North Carolina: Jennifer Thompson, 22, was attacked in her home and made an effort to study the attacker's face. She picked Ronald Cotton from a photo lineup and then a live lineup; a detective told her she had picked the same man both times. She was certain. Cotton was convicted in January 1985 and sentenced to life. DNA testing cleared him and identified another man; he was exonerated on 30 June 1995 after more than ten years. The two later wrote a book together, *Picking Cotton* (2009). | Innocence Project case page; AP feature "The Perfect Witness" | verified. Not used: the 1987 retrial and exact sentence (unconfirmed) |
| D17 | Tell a witness "Good, you identified the suspect" and their confidence rises, along with their memory of how good a view they had. A later pooled analysis of about 7,000 participants found the effect robust. | Wells & Bradfield 1998, *J. Applied Psychology* 83, 360–376; 2014 meta-analysis | verified |
| D18 | The nuance: in a first, clean test (fair lineup, administrator who doesn't know the suspect, confidence recorded on the spot) a witness's confidence does track accuracy. Confidence stated later, including at trial, does not. | Wixted & Wells 2017, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 18 | verified |

## Act 5 · The second trap (misleading words after the event)
| ID | Claim | Source | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| D14 | People watched slides of a car accident and were later asked a question that named the wrong road sign. On a test, those who were misled picked the right slide 41% of the time; those who were not, 75%. A 2025 replication found the effect again. | Loftus, Miller & Burns 1978, *J. Exp. Psychol.: Human Learning and Memory* 4(1), 19–31; Wiechert et al. 2025 | verified via reviews; ⚠ sample size not confirmed, so the study card shows none until checked |
| D13 | Asked how fast the cars were going when they "smashed into" each other, people gave higher speeds (40.8 mph) than when the word was "contacted" (31.8 mph). A week later, 16 of 50 in the "smashed" group said they had seen broken glass, against 7 of 50 for "hit". There was no broken glass. | Loftus & Palmer 1974, *J. Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior* | verified |

## Handling
- **Real people:** Jennifer Thompson-Cannino and Ronald Cotton are living and tell this story publicly. Credited photos only, framed
  neutrally; the other man is not named. Elizabeth Loftus is quoted with attribution.
- **Sensitive material:** the 1984 crime is described as an attack, without detail. Challenger is shown plainly and briefly.
- **What the film must not imply:** that witnesses or victims lie, or that memory is worthless. D18 and D11 carry the other side.
- **Not advice:** nothing here is legal or medical advice.
