# Are we automating racism?

Evidence labels used below:
- **[V] Verified**: stated in a cited source (transcript, interview, companion article, credits).
- **[I] Interpretation**: an informed production inference from verified material, reasoned not observed.
- **[O] Ours**: an original technique we derive for our channels.
Timestamps appear only when a source (transcript, chapter list) supplies them.

## 1. Subject and central question
- [V] Why we think technology is neutral, and how machine-learning systems come to reproduce social bias (Vox description as summarised; Vox tweet says Fong explains how machine learning works and how that produces biased algorithms).
- [V] Examples include image-cropping algorithms, sensors and beauty filters (Popular Sociology summary), and racial disparity in healthcare priority scores (Racism and Technology Center).

## 2. Opening technique
- [V] The episode opens with Fong testing Twitter's automatic image-cropping algorithm herself, with two colleagues (Racism and Technology Center).
- [V] Context: Twitter introduced saliency-based cropping in 2018; users reported it favoured lighter-skinned faces; Twitter later said it found no bias in its own analyses but acknowledged potential for harm and changed the feature (PhoneArena).
- [I] It opens on a live, repeatable experiment the viewer can understand without any theory: a personal test of a public product, before any explanation.

## 3. Narrative structure
- [V] Order reported by the Racism and Technology Center: (1) the hands-on Twitter cropping test; (2) a conversation with Ruha Benjamin, author of *Race After Technology*, about racial disparity in healthcare priority scores; (3) a closing turn from "how do we remove bias" to whether some algorithms should be used at all.
- [V] Between these, the episode explains how machine learning works (Vox tweet).
- [I] Shape: personal experiment, then mechanism (how ML learns from data), then high-stakes case (healthcare), then reframed question. The healthcare segment very likely draws on the widely reported 2019 Obermeyer et al. study of a commercial risk algorithm, but the video's exact source was NOT verified.

## 4. Narration characteristics
- [V] Host-led and first-person: Fong appears as host and runs the experiment herself (Racism and Technology Center; Vox tweet).
- [V] In a 2022 interview Fong said Glad You Asked gives more room for pacing and breathers than Vox's denser explainers (interview as summarised by search).
- [I] The title question ("Are we...?") and the inclusive "we" set an inquiring rather than accusatory tone; inferred from the title and description.

## 11. What makes it effective
- [V] About 2 million views by August 2021 (Racism and Technology Center).
- [V] Hands-on proof first, expert framing second, ethical reframe last (Racism and Technology Center ordering).
- [I] The ending reframe (whether to use a tool at all, not just how to fix it) gives a 22-minute piece a real argumentative climax rather than a summary.

## 12. What we can adapt (original equivalents, never copies)
- [O] **Run the test on camera (faceless):** open with a reproducible experiment on a public system, shown as a screen capture with our hands or cursor only: input, output, repeat. The viewer sees the effect before hearing any theory.
- [O] **Three-tier escalation:** a trivial everyday case (a photo crop), then the mechanism, then a high-stakes case in the same mechanism (health, money, justice). Works for any systemic topic.
- [O] **Reframe ending:** end long films by changing the question (from "how do we fix X?" to "should X exist / who decides?"), attributed to a named expert; meets our "show the other side" rule if we add the defender's view.
- [O] **Breathers by design:** in 20+ minute films, schedule a 5 to 8 second visual pause after each major reveal (within our 4.5 s still / 7 s building-shot limits by using a slow building shot), so density doesn't exhaust viewers.
- [O] **Company response card:** when testing a product, show the company's documented response with date and source.
