# When white supremacists overthrew a government

Evidence labels used below:
- **[V] Verified**: stated in a cited source (transcript, interview, companion article, credits).
- **[I] Interpretation**: an informed production inference from verified material, reasoned not observed.
- **[O] Ours**: an original technique we derive for our channels.
Timestamps appear only when a source (transcript, chapter list) supplies them.

## 1. Subject and central question
- [V] The November 1898 coup in Wilmington, North Carolina: a mob of about 2,000 white men expelled Black and white political leaders, destroyed the Black-owned newspaper and killed dozens or more (vox.com page; transcript).
- [V] The film frames it as "the violent overthrow of a democratically elected government", something "we don't usually find in American history" (transcript, short phrases; chapter at 0:42).
- [V] Missing Chapter revisits underreported moments of the past to give context to the present (vox.com page).

## 2. Opening technique
- [V] Cold open on suppression, not on the violence: a local voice recalls being refused by the librarian when asking about 1898, told the material was kept "under lock and key" (transcript; short phrase).
- [V] Then the host: the story still is not widely known, and the site is now an empty patch of grass (transcript).
- [I] The opening turns the act of researching into the hook: the viewer learns first that someone tried to hide this.

## 3. Narrative structure
- [V] Chapter list (rosetta.to): untitled opening 0:00 → the violent overthrow of a democratically elected government 0:42 → Black men threatening white women 4:29 → the White Declaration of Independence 7:15.
- [V] Order in the transcript: Wilmington as a city of Black prosperity and office-holding → the parties of 1898 and the Fusion coalition → the Democratic plan to split white Populists from Black voters → press campaign and racist cartoons → Rebecca Felton's speech and Alex Manly's editorial → intimidation and the Red Shirts → the election → the declaration and ultimatum → burning of the Daily Record (quote stamped 7:26) → forced resignations, Waddell as mayor → the killings and the flight from the city → Jim Crow and the end of Black representation (9:53) → textbooks that praised a perpetrator → present-day voting fights (11:46-11:54).
- [I] Three-part shape: how it was built, how it was done, how it was erased and what remains.

## 4. Narration characteristics
- [V] Host-producer narration with historians and a descendant-community voice (Cynthia Brown, a church historian) (transcript).
- [V] The film explains that the parties of 1898 were roughly reversed from today, to prevent a modern partisan misreading (transcript).
- [V] Vox published a correction (earlier pass, search summary): the film says "descendants" where it means "ancestors". The transcript confirms the word as spoken ("whose descendants were in Wilmington back in 1898").
- [V] Casualty figures are attributed and hedged: one historian sees 40 to 60 clear fatalities and believes the toll was higher (transcript).

## 5. Visual techniques
- [V] The narration directs the viewer to documents: a poster of the 1889 North Carolina House with Black Republican members "here at the bottom"; 1898 photos where X marks show where the first Black residents were killed; a 1933 and a 1978 textbook; a Democratic image of armed Black men called a gross misrepresentation (transcript).
- [V] It also points to places: "this is where it once stood" for the Daily Record site, and the empty patch of grass in the opening (transcript).
- [I] So the film is built on archival documents shown on screen and present-day location footage. Inferred from deictic narration; the framing and treatment are not observed.

## 7. Footage selection
- [V] Archival: the 1889 legislature poster, racist political cartoons from a Democratic newspaper, the declaration as printed in the next day's paper (a preserved copy at Brown's church), 1898 photographs, celebratory booklets, two textbooks (transcript).
- [V] Present-day: interviews with historians and Cynthia Brown; news clips on North Carolina's voter-ID law and racial gerrymandering near the end (transcript).
- [V] The vox.com page points readers to LeRae Umfleet's work and the state commission's report for further reading.

## 11. What makes it effective
- [V] It became a reference: Open Culture embedded it and stressed the librarian detail; HNN republished it; a Brown University resource collection lists it (Open Culture opened; others earlier pass).
- [V] About 4.6 million views (rosetta.to listing per search; the opened page confirms date and length).

- [I] A living voice connected to the place turns an archival story into a present-tense one.

## 12. What we can adapt (original equivalents, never copies)
- [O] **Open on the locked drawer.** When a story was suppressed, open on the act of suppression (a refused request, a sealed file, a missing page), shown as a document, before the event itself.
- [O] **The relabel.** Show what the event was called at the time and what evidence now shows, with dates and sources for both labels.
- [O] **The object that was destroyed.** Pick one physical thing lost in the story (a press, a ledger, a building) and explain what it did for people.
- [O] **Old textbook as exhibit.** Quote how earlier official histories told the story, as dated documents, to show how memory was shaped.
- [O] **Public correction protocol.** If an error is found after release, pin a timestamped correction (time, wrong word, right word) and log it in SEEN/dossier.
