# The real reason Egypt is moving its capital

Evidence labels used below:
- **[V] Verified**: stated in a cited source (transcript, interview, companion article, credits).
- **[I] Interpretation**: an informed production inference from verified material, reasoned not observed.
- **[O] Ours**: an original technique we derive for our channels.
Timestamps appear only when a source (transcript, chapter list) supplies them.

## 1. Subject and central question
- [V] In 2015 Egypt announced it would move its capital about 50 km from Cairo into the desert. The official reason is Cairo's overpopulation; the film asks why the population is still called a crisis after decades of new desert cities, and what the real reason is (transcript). Correction: the earlier draft said 40-45 km from search summaries; the narration says about 50.
- [V] Its answer: the new capital moves the government out of reach of the crowds that paralysed it in 2011 (transcript, closing lines).
- [V] Ellis says the film did not come from a breaking story; it came from asking two questions: the site is far away, and earlier cities outside Cairo were built before, so did they work? (Storybench, paraphrased).

## 2. Opening technique
- [V] The narration opens by pointing at places: it names Cairo, then "this building" (the Parliament, used for over 150 years) and "this public square" (site of revolutions), then the move to a "patch of empty desert" and where the new palace, parliament and square will go (transcript).
- [V] The two questions are stated within the first tenth of the transcript, before any history (transcript; position measured by word count).
- [I] The deictic words suggest the opening shows the real buildings and the desert site, probably on a map or in aerial imagery, given Ellis's map-first format. Not observed.

## 3. Narrative structure
- [V] Chapter titles (rosetta.to, untimed): introduction to Cairo and the new capital → density and history → informal neighbourhoods and government blame → past attempts at new cities → the real reason (political control) → redesigning Cairo for control.
- [V] Order in the transcript: official claim → density figures (153,000 people per km² at its peak) → how Cairo grew along the Nile, era by era → informal neighbourhoods built on farmland → the state's "slum" framing, contested by the historian → Nasr City in the 1950s and eight desert cities from 1976, mostly expensive and car-dependent → the new capital's housing aimed at a "moneyed class" → 2011, Tahrir Square, the march to the palace 10 km away → Sisi's 2013 seizure of power and crackdown → street widening, bridges and the Tahrir redesign → the government moving almost 50 km away.
- [V] The official reason is tested against history before the alternative is offered; the political answer starts only about three-quarters of the way in (transcript; position by word count).
- [I] Structure is a hypothesis test: claim, precedent, who benefits, alternative motive.

## 4. Narration characteristics
- [V] Ellis writes, voices, animates and cuts his own Atlas films, with a story editor on the script (Storybench, Belt and Road interview; general description of his role).
- [V] One expert voice carries the counter-argument: architectural historian Mohamed Elshahed, who calls the overpopulation story a "dangerous narrative" (transcript; short phrase).
- [V] The motive is stated as the film's conclusion but hedged in places ("some experts say", "that may be the point") (transcript).
- [I] The argument is made through geography (distance, location, who lives where) more than through quotes.

## 5. Visual techniques
- [V] Ellis describes the Atlas layout as maps with animation on top, news clips, photos and data visualisation, and says toggling between the map and what is happening on the ground helps viewers (Storybench).
- [V] For this film he traced historical maps by hand from old books; he says that work was not geolocated data (Storybench).
- [V] The film took five weeks to make (Storybench).
- [I] The era-by-era growth of Cairo ("the Ottomans built these areas... the British added these suburbs") is narrated deictically, consistent with traced historical layers on a map. Not observed.

## 6. Graphics and animation
- [V] Ellis builds Atlas animation himself in After Effects, with notes from an art director (Storybench, Belt and Road interview).
- [V] Numbers in the script that a graphic would carry: 100 million Egyptians, about 20% in Greater Cairo, peak density of 153,000 per km², 60% of Cairo living in informal areas, eight desert cities from 1976, 40 planned bridges, more than 800 killed in the 2013 raid (transcript). ## 11. What makes it effective
- [V] About 3.5 million views (rosetta.to listing).
- [V] Ellis's own explanation of the format: Vox makes news interesting by telling the story first, with a beginning, middle and end (Storybench).
- [I] It turns a planning story into a power story using the variable maps show best, distance (50 km against the 10 km march of 2011).
- [I] Testing the official reason against history first makes the motive claim feel earned.

## 12. What we can adapt (original equivalents, never copies)
- [O] **Official vs measured.** State the official reason on screen as a quote card with source; then measure it with one map variable (distance, elevation, who lives within X km) and show the result.
- [O] **Precedent row.** Before claiming a motive, show earlier attempts of the same kind and how they turned out; a row of past cases is evidence, the motive is interpretation (label it).
- [O] **Two distances.** When the claim is about access, put two sourced distances side by side (then vs now) in the signature yellow.
- [O] **Hand-traced history layers.** Where no GIS data exists, trace dated historical maps and label each layer with its source and date in the dossier, saying on screen that it is approximate.
- [O] **Who benefits filter.** For any infrastructure story, add a beat that shows who can afford or reach the new thing, with sourced figures.
