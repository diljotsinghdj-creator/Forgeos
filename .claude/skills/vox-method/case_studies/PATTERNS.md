### 1. Opening moves
- **A number before any story.** HAI opens on paired outcome statistics (infant mortality, income per head). The SYR
  description leads with the death and displacement toll. The GER description leads with "435 districts".
  Supported by: HAI [V], SYR [V, description], GER [V, description].
- **Correcting a myth.** ISR's description reframes an "ancient" conflict as only about a century old. The thesis is a
  correction of what the viewer believes. Supported by: ISR [V, description].
- **"Says X, does Y."** CHN's description sets China's denial (the islands are not bases) against its actions. SYR's caption excerpts
  show outside powers mostly hitting someone other than their stated enemy. Supported by: CHN [V], SYR [V, caption excerpts].

### 2. Structures

### 3. Visual modes chosen for which kinds of claims (series-level evidence, frames not seen)
- **Maps for "where" and "who controls what".** Vox Atlas is defined by TheTVDB as a map-led series showing where conflicts
  happen (CHN, TAL). One search summary reported colour-coded faction icons on a map in SYR; a second search did not
  confirm it.
- **Archival footage for "where this came from".** PUT's description lists an archive.org clip at 2:42 (the only

- Vox explainers in general mix graphics, animation and news footage (Nieman Storyboard, 2016).

### 6. Sourcing and transparency (strong pattern)
- **Timestamped source lists in the description.** PUT lists each footage source with the timestamp where it appears,
  plus further reading [V].
- **A written companion to the video.** GER links a modular Vox card stack, "Gerrymandering, explained" [V].
- **Local audiences as a source network.** Borders used the host's Facebook page as a community hub during shoots and
  hoped locals would become sources (HAI, Nieman Lab) [V].

### 7. Packaging and titles
- **The title is the question or the promise:** "Who is fighting and why" (SYR), "Why China is building..." (CHN),
  "...explained" (TAL), "How politicians rig elections" (GER), "From spy to president" (PUT, an arc), "a brief, simple
  history" (ISR, a promise about format), "Divided island: How..." (HAI, an image followed by a question). All seven do this.
- **Runtime follows the subject, not the slot.** Atlas usually runs about 5 minutes (CHN). It stretched to about 14 when
  history and the present both had to be covered (TAL). Borders ran 10 to 15 minutes with field reporting (HAI). The
  gerrymandering topic was later scaled up to a 21 to 25 minute Netflix episode.
- **Explain after the news, not during it.** TAL came out about three months after the fall of Kabul. Press Gazette quotes Vox
  saying it does not rush information out the door. PUT tied an evergreen biography to the news hook of the day.

### 8. Differences between videos (what changed with the subject)
- **Contested history versus mechanism.** ISR (a contested history) drew detailed critiques from advocacy groups on its
  compression and framing. GER (a mechanism) drew reposts from advocacy groups instead. The more a film compresses
  a contested history, the more each omission is read as taking a side.
- **The host's presence grows with the need for fieldwork.** GER is carried by a senior presenter's authority. HAI has a
  travelling reporter-host. Atlas pieces are defined by maps rather than by a host.
- **Length grows with time depth.** Disputes in a single snapshot (CHN) fit in 5 minutes. Stories that need an origin plus a
  present (TAL, HAI) run 14 to 15.
- **Exposure to criticism grows with field claims.** HAI drew local accuracy complaints about a historical figure.
  Place-based films get checked by the people who live in that place.

### 9. The three strongest patterns
1. **Open on a hard number or a corrected belief, then ask the question the title already posed.** Seen in HAI, SYR, GER,
   ISR, and implied by every title.
2. **Choose the structure by the kind of claim.** Use a cast list for many actors, chronology for origins, problem-proof-fix for
   policy, and a controlled comparison for gaps between neighbours. Use maps for "where", archive for "where from" and
   fieldwork for "to whom".
3. **Make the sourcing visible in the package.** Timestamped footage lists, companion card stacks, and a local source
   network. This fits our dossier and evidence-first gates directly.

### 1. Opening moves
- **Correct a belief or state a surprising measure, then ask why.** IP16 opens by recasting an "ancient" conflict as about 100 years old [V]. HT17 opens on a gap in health and poverty figures [V]. NV25 opens on a count (600+ towns) [V]. RU14 is framed as "less obvious than you think" [V]. *Pattern:* the first beat resets the viewer's assumption with one number or one date.
- **Official claim first, then test it.** EG22 ("The real reason...") states the overcrowding rationale and then tests it [V summary].
- **Concrete datable event as the starting point.** SY15 starts from the 2011 protests [V].

### 2. Structures
- **Chronological accumulation:** SY15 (actors added one at a time), IP16 (a century in order) [V].
- **Immerse, then ask:** HT17 places "why so different?" at 7:00 of ~16 min, after on-location immersion [V timestamp; I reading].
- **Hypothesis test:** EG22 (official reason → precedent of empty desert cities → who the housing serves → political reading) [V summaries].
- **Erasure/relabel arc:** WM19 (event → burial and heroisation of perpetrators → present-day keeper) [V description; I shape].
- **Motive tracing:** RU14 (2012 turn → 2014 action → no clear end) [V summary].
- **Road-trip cycle:** NV25 (stops along a drive illustrate boom-and-bust) [V trip; I beat mapping].

### 3. Visual modes chosen for which kinds of claims
- **Maps for spatial and power claims:** EG22's argument is distance from the protest square [V summaries], built in a master-map workflow by Sam Ellis [V Storybench/Google Earth]. IP16's highlighted historical map is where critics aimed [V Daily Wire].
- **On-location footage for lived claims:** HT17 (boat crossings, shared market, merchant interview) [V]; NV25 (road trip, sites) [V].
- **Archival and witness voice for buried-history claims:** WM19 (Daily Record destruction, church historian) [V]; photos used not confirmed.
- **Explainer-only voice for fast-moving news:** RU14 (editor explains) [V].
- Inference [I]: Vox matches the visual mode to the type of claim. Space → map. Experience → location. Memory → archive plus witness.

### 4. Maps and archival handling
- Ellis: maps are a strong video format. Start from visuals at surface level, build in After Effects, and link sequences to a master world map for zooms [V Storybench; Medium].
- Map crops and highlights are read as claims. IP16's yellow highlight was disputed for what it left out [V Daily Wire]. Place names were disputed too [V ADL].
- Archival corrections are public. WM19 published a timestamped wording correction [V]. A reader disputed HT17's indemnity figure [V Reddit, unverified claim].

### 5. Transition devices
- Mostly unverified. [I] candidates: the master-map zoom between scales (EG22 workflow), the physical route as connective tissue (HT17 border road, NV25 drive), and date stamps in timeline films (SY15, IP16).

### 6. Sound

### 7. Packaging and titles
- **Question or promise titles:** "Why..." (RU14, NV25), "Who is fighting and why" (SY15), "The real reason..." (EG22) [V titles].
- **Scope promise in the title:** "in 2 minutes" (RU14), "brief, simple" (IP16) [V]. IP16 shows the risk: "simple" on a contested topic invites audits.
- **Flat, startling declarative:** "When white supremacists overthrew a government" (WM19) [V].
- **Two-places contrast:** "Divided island... two worlds" (HT17) [V].
- **Series brands carry the method:** Borders = travel along a line; Atlas = maps; Missing Chapter = buried history with a host [V series descriptions].
- **Re-cuts and spin-offs:** SY15 re-made in 2017 [V]; NV25 cut into a Short [V].

### 8. Differences: what changed with the subject
- **News-driven geopolitics (RU14, SY15):** short and analyst-voiced, with an updatable timeline and no location shoot. Value is clarity under time pressure.
- **Contested history (IP16):** ten minutes of chronology. Wording, verbs, maps and names became the battleground. Needs a balance audit more than spectacle.
- **Place-based geography (HT17, NV25):** location shoots, a host or producer on the road, local voices. The route is the structure.
- **Buried history (WM19):** a relabel arc with a descendant-community voice and a correction discipline.
- **Policy geography (EG22):** map-built argument, about five weeks of graphics work by one producer [V summary], with motive offered as interpretation over measured distance.
- **Over time:** the 2014-2016 videos are analyst explainers with no field work. In 2017-2019 the series formats arrive, with travel, hosts and archival witnesses. By 2022-2025 there are specialised formats (Atlas maps) and sponsored travel pieces with disclosure and Short cut-downs. [I from dates and formats of this sample of 7; not a census]

### 9. Three strongest patterns (best evidence)
1. **Reset-the-assumption opening.** A number, a date or an official claim challenges what the viewer thinks, then the "why" follows (IP16, HT17, NV25, EG22, RU14).
2. **Visual mode follows the claim type.** Maps for space and power, location for lived experience, archive and witness for buried history (EG22, HT17, NV25, WM19).
3. **Maps and words are audited as claims.** Crops, highlights, place names, verbs and historical sums get scrutinised, and corrections are public (IP16, WM19, HT17). For us: log every map crop, label and figure in the dossier with its source.

### 1. Opening moves
- **The question is the title, and the opening promises an answer.** "Why is...", "Can...", "Are we..." and "...explained" appear in AR, CE, EX, FW, TW and AA. [V] from the titles.
- **Open on something familiar or ordinary, not on a statistic.** HS opens on a cartoon character's jobs; EX on diapers, food and rent; TW on a fantasy-drama analogy; CE on a real viewer's question. [V] from the descriptions and transcript excerpts.
- **Reverse a problem the audience thinks is solved.** AA starts from 2015 image captioning and asks what running it backwards would give. [V] from the ytscribe excerpt.
- **Show that the fear is old.** FW reaches back to automation anxiety in the late 1950s and early 1960s. [V] from the rosetta.to excerpts.

### 2. Structures
- **A line-up of competing explanations, each tested on the same object.** EX tests three causes against diapers; FW sets futurists against economists. [V]
- **Mechanism, then escalation, then the bystander effect.** TW goes from tariff to retaliation to trade war, then to the third parties who gain. [V]
- **Lineage, then breakthrough, then mechanism, then stakes.** AA follows that order. [V] for the early beats; [I] for the order of the later ones.
- **A cheap demonstration first, then a consequential system.** AR runs a cropping test, then turns to health-care scores. [V] for both elements; [I] for the order.
- **Data set, method, placement, national trend.** HS narrows a list to 100 jobs, prices each one, and places Homer on the income distribution. [V]
- **Question, scale, growth, bottlenecks, then a direct answer.** CE follows this path. [V] for the elements.
- **Ending:** CE gives a verdict. FW ends on an open question about distribution, and EX ends on remedies matched to causes. [V] for each.

### 3. How data and charts carry claims
- **Time series against a baseline, with the shocks marked.** In FW, employment of prime-age Americans stays near 80% outside recessions. [V] for the claim; [I] that it was charted.
- **The counter-signal indicator.** In FW, slowing productivity growth is used to argue against an automation surge. [V]
- **Input cost against final price.** In EX, the rise in input costs does not fully explain the rise in diaper prices. [V] for the claim; [I] for the chart.
- **One table holds the case, and it is published.** HS puts 100 jobs and their salaries in a table in the companion article. [V]
- **A share of the total that is projected to double.** In CE, data centres were about 2% of world electricity in 2022, projected to double by 2026. [V]
- **Unit equivalence.** CE gives data-centre power as a number of households, and the figure varies between sources. [V] This is a warning to verify conversions.

### 4. How abstract systems are made visible
- **A borrowed analogy world.** TW uses fictional houses for trading nations. [V]
- **A proxy person.** HS uses a character as a stand-in for the median worker. [V]
- **The system's own output as evidence.** AA shows generated images; AR shows crops. [V] that the subjects were these outputs; [I] for the on-screen treatment.
- **Physical constraints made concrete.** CE shows that the sun sets and that the queue to connect to the grid takes years. [V]

### 8. Packaging and titles
- **"X, explained."** Used by AA, TW and FW. [V]
- **A plain question in the second person or the collective "we".** Used by EX, CE and AR. [V]
- **A pop-culture hook in the title or description.** HS names the character; TW names the analogy in its description. [V]
- **A companion article or podcast.** HS has a companion article; CE belongs to a podcast-linked series (Explain It to Me); AR is a host-led series. [V]
- **Short summary lines in the descriptions.** AA's describes the internet as a paintbrush; EX's lists diapers, food and rent. [V]

### 9. Differences: what changed with the subject and the era
- **Host presence.** The technology and society pieces (AR, 2021) use an on-camera host who joins in the experiment. The economics pieces read as narrated explainers. [V] for AR's host; [I] for the others.
- **Length.** Short mechanism pieces such as TW (about 5 minutes) use one analogy. Mid-length pieces (about 9 to 13.5 minutes: FW, EX, CE, AA) test several explanations or tell a lineage. The long series episode (AR, about 22 minutes) adds an experiment and an interview. [V] for the lengths.
- **Source of the topic.** Topics shifted from news pegs (TW in 2018: the tariff threats) and pop culture (HS in 2016) to audience questions (CE in 2024). [V]
- **Stance.** Economic debates are framed as contests between theories (FW, EX). Technology harms are framed as testable claims with a fix (AR). The tool explainer (AA) leans on history and mechanism. [V] for the framings.
- **Allegations.** EX's markup theory and AR's claims of bias are both allegations against companies. The sources do not show whether the companies' responses were included. Our rules require us to show them.

### 10. Three strongest transferable patterns
1. **One ordinary object or proxy carries the macro story.** HS, EX and TW each do this, and CE does it through its household equivalence.
2. **Test the claim against a baseline or counter-signal instead of asserting it.** FW, EX and AR each do this, AR through its own experiment.
3. **The title is a question, the scope is tight, and the answer is stated.** CE, EX, AR and TW, plus FW's "both sides" framing, follow this pattern.
