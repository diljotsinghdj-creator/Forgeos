## 1. What the studied craft shows

- [V] **Pick stories that are poorly suited to text,** ones that only become clear when you see them (P1, snippet;
  confirmed on full pages by P9 and P42: the founding rule was to use video only when a story has distinctly visual
  elements). Some stories were chosen because they can't be explained "without drawing it on a napkin" (P9).
- [V] **No desks, no anchors, few talking heads.** Video is its own medium, not a broadcast or an article read aloud
  (P1, snippet). The team began by avoiding on-camera talent and leaning on motion graphics and narration (P18).
- [V] **Late, not first.** On big ongoing news, the video usually arrives a week or two after the event. The aim is
  the most meaningful piece, not the fastest (P2). One case study came out about three months after the event it
  explains (the-taliban-explained-2021).
- [V] **Fast when the work is done in advance.** The black-hole video went up about an hour after the press
  conference because it adapted an earlier explainer with a reserved slot for the real image (P3,
  black-hole-photo-big-deal-2019).
- [V] **Evergreen library over news spikes.** Explainers are seen as long-life content, and the long-form spin-off
  was framed as building a library (P4; P41: Netflix was wanted as an evergreen archive; P5 snippet only).
- [V] **Trivial-looking subjects made nontrivial.** One producer's pieces on fonts, siding and graffiti reached
  millions by finding the system behind the small thing (P12).
- [V] **Experts shape the nuance,** for example that infrastructure money is a loan to be repaid, not aid (P9).
  Long-form pieces give first-hand
  guides room to explain their own reasoning and say what is unknown (PATTERNS: science/culture §5).
- [V] **The outsider problem.** A travelling reporter can read as "parachuting in"; the series answered with local
  source networks and engagement reporting (P6, P7).
- [V] **Compression on contested topics draws audits.** A "brief, simple history" of a contested conflict drew
  detailed critiques of its maps, names and verbs from several sides (israel-palestine-brief-simple-history).
- [V] **Public corrections.** A buried-history video published a timestamped wording correction
  (when-white-supremacists-overthrew-a-government). A science producer named the flaw in her own video (P3).
  Content normally stays live with editor's notes and corrections; readers report errors by email (P10).
- [V] **The pitch is a package, not a topic:** a headline, the angle, the list of visual evidence that will prove it,
  and the people who will be interviewed to fact-check it. Finding the counter-arguments is part of the job; how the
  animation looks comes last (P16).
- [V] **Experts are asked for two things:** an explanation the producer can translate, and visual evidence or data
  (a think tank's database of ports and railways). Ask them what nobody is covering yet (P9).
- [V] **One key document per film:** a producer looks for the "Rosetta stone" source that carries the narrative, and
  treats research as finished when new sources only repeat facts already found (P12).
- [V] **Neutrality is built shot by shot:** which words are highlighted and which photo is used (a flattering or
  unflattering angle) can tilt a profile, so both went through several iterations; candidate "moments" were checked
  against the experts interviewed (P15).
- [V] **Review is heavy:** weeks of research and interviews, then six rounds of review (P33). A field series added an
  assistant producer for about six weeks to fact-check (P17).

- [V] **Evidence, then explanation, as the house style:** Johnny Harris described the Vox style as frequent
  first-hand visual anchors that show the subject, followed by a quick, succinct explanation, unlike broadcast
  segments built on long anchor talk (P2).
- [V] **Ideas start as questions:** the editorial director says the best brainstorms bring questions to the table,
  not answers ("what do you want to know about?"), and that the aim is to go deeper than a simple
  question-and-explanation (P2).
- [V] **Style and substance together.** The team of 20 includes 12 reporter-producers who report and edit, plus
  researchers, designers and story editors. Some staff come from art or social backgrounds and are trained in
  journalism (P2).

## 2. The story test [O]

A topic becomes a film only if it passes all five:

| Test | Question | Fail looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **Visual necessity** | Does a picture do explanatory work prose cannot? | It would be just as good as a podcast |
| **One question** | Is there one question a curious viewer would type? | "A video about X" |
| **A surprising answer** | Does the answer reset an assumption? | The answer is what everyone already thinks |
| **Native material** | Can we show the thing itself (documents, places, data, footage)? | Only stock footage exists |
| **Why now / why care** | Is there a news peg, a cost to the viewer, or a hidden system in their daily life? | Only "it's interesting" |

Scoring: a pass on all five → pitch it. Four → pitch with the weak test named. Three or fewer → it's a topic, not a
film yet. Keep researching or drop it.

## 3. Framing the question [O, drawn from PATTERNS §1 and §7 in every category]

- **Reset the assumption.** The strongest openings correct a belief with one number, one date or one official claim,
  then ask why (PATTERNS: history/geography §1, politics §1). Write the assumption the viewer holds, then the fact that
  breaks it.
- **Shape of a good question:** "Why does X happen when you'd expect Y?" · "How did X become Y?" · "Who actually
  decides X?" · "What would it take to X?" · "Was X really Y?" (forensic).
- **Scope tightly.** One place, one object, one mechanism, one decision. Zoom out to the system only after the
  specific case has been earned.
- **"Says X, does Y."** For institutions and governments, set the official claim against the record
  (politics §1, why-china-builds-islands-south-china-sea-2017).
- **One checkable object carries the abstraction:** a diaper's price, a cropped photo, a pebble's position, an
  instrument (PATTERNS: econ/tech §10, sci/culture §9). Find the object before writing the script.

## 4. Making the complicated accessible [O, with [V] support]

1. **Learn it first, deeply.** One producer taught herself music theory with expert help before designing the
   metaphors (P16). Our equivalent is the dossier (research before structure). About half of a researcher-led
   producer's time goes on research (P19).
2. **Sequence the reveal.** Show why something is hard before showing the result, so the result lands (P3).
3. **Define by showing.** Every technical term arrives with a picture of what it refers to, in the same breath.
4. **Stair-step abstraction.** Concrete case → named pattern → mechanism → system → implication. Never start at the
   system.
5. **One metaphor per idea, labelled as a metaphor,** and never reused from the last film (BRAIN_LOG).
6. **Choose the structure by the kind of claim** (politics §2): a cast list for many actors, chronology for origins,
   problem → proof → fix for policy, a controlled comparison for gaps between neighbours, forensic steps for "was it
   real?", lineage → breakthrough → mechanism → stakes for a technology.
7. **Test, don't assert.** Put a claim against a baseline, a counter-signal or an experiment (econ/tech §2).

## 5. Information and entertainment [O]

- Entertainment here means **curiosity and payoff,** not jokes. Open loops, reveals, a turn, a satisfying answer.
- Each chapter must change what the viewer thinks: a new fact, a complication or a reversal.
- Density: one new idea per ~10–15 seconds in explanatory stretches; slow down for evidence and for human
  consequence. Long films need breathers (P18).
- Humour only if it comes from the material itself (an absurd document, a real contradiction), never a gag that
  undercuts a serious subject (P8).

## 6. Authority and credibility [O]

- **Show your sources on screen:** a source line on evidence and data, and badges on anything that isn't plain fact.
- **Visible sourcing in the package:** a timestamped source list in the description and a companion source document
  (politics §6, from-spy-to-president-putin-2017).
- **Primary material is the star:** documents, records and data come first; commentary second.
- **The other side, shown fairly:** allegations attributed, responses included or their absence stated. Contested
  histories get a balance audit of maps, names and verbs before release.
- **Say what's unknown.** Honest uncertainty is an ending, not a weakness (famous-war-photo-staged-errol-morris-2020).
- **Expert voice without faces (faceless channels):** credited, paraphrased statements on source cards, or the
  expert's published document, annotated.

## 7. Corrections [O]

- Any error found after release gets a correction line in the description (after the chapters) and, if possible,
  a "Correction:" line with the error's timestamp in the description, which makes YouTube show an info pop-up at that
  moment linking to it (P11).
- Log the correction in BRAIN_LOG (CreatorForge) with the rule that would have prevented it.

## 8. Our editorial checklist (run before the script is locked)

- [ ] The five story tests pass (§2), written in `question.md`.
- [ ] The opening resets a named assumption with one dossier fact.
- [ ] The structure matches the kind of claim (§4.6).
- [ ] There is one checkable object or test that carries the abstraction.
- [ ] Every figure, date and name has a claim id; every allegation is attributed with the other side shown.
- [ ] Contested topics: maps, names and verbs audited for balance; DISPUTED badges where they apply.
- [ ] What is unknown is said.
- [ ] Nothing about the film needs the viewer to already know the topic.
